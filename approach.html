Treatment approach

A thoughtful, integrated, whole-person approach.

  • Our team of physiotherapists brings decades of knowledge, hands-on skill and passion to their practice.
  • We look beyond symptoms to understand their underlying causes and contributing factors.

Pelvic health

How is our pelvic health physiotherapy approach integrative?

Pelvic health is connected to the function of the whole body. Pelvic symptoms don’t always have a single cause, and the contributing factors may extend beyond the pelvic floor itself.

What makes our approach integrative is the combination of our therapists’ diverse skills, clinical experience and areas of training, together with our commitment to collaborating closely as a team. We look beyond individual symptoms to understand how different systems, movement patterns and experiences may interact. Your assessment and treatment plan are individualized to your needs, goals and preferences, and may draw on some or all of the following approaches.

Read more: the six approaches

  1. Pelvic Health Physiotherapy

    Pelvic health physiotherapy helps us assess the function, coordination, strength and relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles, along with other factors relevant to your symptoms. When clinically appropriate, an internal vaginal and/or anorectal assessment may provide additional information. An internal assessment is always optional. You can ask questions, defer the assessment or choose not to have one. We take the time to discuss your concerns and explain each step so that you feel informed, respected and in control of your care.

    Your treatment may include pelvic floor muscle training or relaxation, movement retraining, education, hands-on techniques and strategies to support everyday activities.

  2. Orthopedic Physiotherapy

    The spine, hips, pelvis, rib cage and lower limbs all contribute to how we move and manage physical demands. Pain, stiffness or altered movement in one area may influence how other areas function. For example, reduced hip mobility may affect your walking pattern or how you move during everyday activities, and may contribute to low back or pelvic discomfort and influence how the pelvic floor muscles work. By assessing posture, gait and functional movement patterns, we can explore whether orthopedic factors may be contributing to your pelvic health symptoms.

  3. Integrated Systems Model (Diane Lee)

    An assessment and treatment framework that helps us explore how different parts of the body interact and identify potential key drivers of a person’s symptoms or movement difficulties. For example, foot mechanics may influence lower-limb movement and the way the pelvis and trunk move during walking. Rather than focusing only on the area where you feel pain, this approach encourages us to consider the wider movement system and the relationships between its parts.

  4. Myofascial Mobilization

    Fascia is connective tissue that surrounds and connects muscles and other structures throughout the body. When clinically appropriate, gentle hands-on techniques may be used to address soft-tissue mobility, comfort and movement. For some patients, this may include working with abdominal or perineal scars related to caesarean birth, perineal tears, episiotomies, surgery or previous injury, aiming to improve scar mobility, reduce sensitivity or support more comfortable movement.

  5. Visceral Mobilization

    The pelvic organs — including the bladder, uterus and rectum — sit within a network of surrounding connective tissues and are influenced by the movement and pressure changes of breathing and everyday activities. Visceral mobilization involves gentle, hands-on techniques intended to address the mobility and comfort of these tissues, and may consider the diaphragm, rib cage and related structures. These techniques are used selectively as part of an individualized plan, rather than as a stand-alone solution.

  6. Pain Science & Nervous System Regulation

    Pain is influenced by more than tissue injury alone. The nervous system, previous experiences, stress, sleep and other factors can affect how pain is processed. Sometimes the nervous system remains more sensitive after an initial injury has settled, contributing to persistent pain. Depending on your needs, we may incorporate pain education, breathing strategies, graded exposure to activities, relaxation and mindful movement — at a pace that feels appropriate for you.

Putting the pieces together

Integrative pelvic health physiotherapy is not about using every technique with every patient. It is about understanding your individual presentation, identifying factors that may be contributing to your symptoms and selecting the approaches most relevant to your needs. We work collaboratively with you, adapting your care as your symptoms, goals and progress evolve.

Your symptoms are important. So is the bigger picture.

Trauma-informed care

Care grounded in respect, choice, collaboration and trust.

We believe that feeling safe, heard and respected is an essential part of receiving care. Previous medical experiences, painful examinations, childbirth, surgery or other life experiences can influence how someone feels about physical contact and healthcare. You do not need to share personal experiences to receive thoughtful, respectful care — and you do not need to have experienced trauma to benefit from this approach.

If you ask us to stop, we will stop.

Read more: what does trauma-informed care look like?
  • Listening without judgment. We take time to understand your concerns, goals and preferences, without making assumptions about your body, identity, lifestyle or health choices.
  • Clear communication and informed consent. We explain assessments and treatments, ask permission before hands-on care, welcome questions and respect your right to decline or withdraw consent at any time.
  • Choice and collaboration. You are an active participant. Share what helps you feel comfortable, ask for breaks, request a different approach or have a support person present when appropriate.
  • Inclusive, respectful care. We strive to provide gender-affirming care that respects each person’s identity, privacy, dignity and cultural background, using clear, accessible language.
  • Privacy and comfort. When assessment or treatment involves sensitive areas, we explain each step, discuss options and obtain consent. We use appropriate draping and uncover only the area needed for care.

In pelvic health physiotherapy

Pelvic health physiotherapy may involve personal conversations, physical contact or, when clinically appropriate, an internal vaginal or anorectal examination. An internal examination is not something you must agree to simply because it has been recommended. We explain its potential benefits, discuss alternatives and respect your decision. If you choose to proceed, we check in with you throughout, and you can pause or stop at any time.

Working together at your pace

Trauma-informed care is an ongoing commitment to communication, trust, choice and respect. You deserve care that respects your voice, your choices and your boundaries.

Somatic education

Gentle somatic education for pelvic health.

Developing body awareness, easing tension and exploring movement at your own pace.

Pelvic health involves more than muscle strength alone. Breathing, movement, muscle coordination, pain and previous experiences can all influence how we feel in our bodies. Gentle somatic education focuses on noticing internal sensations and exploring slow, comfortable movements. Rather than focusing on repetitions or perfect technique, the emphasis is on how movement feels and how the body responds.

Read more: how can somatic education help?

For some people, pelvic health concerns involve difficulty relaxing the pelvic floor, persistent muscle tension or challenges coordinating contraction and release. Somatic approaches may complement pelvic health physiotherapy by helping you:

  • Recognize patterns of tension and holding.
  • Explore gentle muscle relaxation and release.
  • Develop awareness of pelvic floor function.
  • Improve coordination between breathing and movement.
  • Build confidence in movement at a comfortable pace.

What might it look like in a session?

  • Breath awareness: noticing how your rib cage, abdomen and pelvic region respond to breathing.
  • Gentle movement: small, comfortable movements without forcing range or effort.
  • Pelvic floor awareness: practising gentle contraction and release when appropriate.
  • Guided attention: noticing sensations and patterns of tension without judgment.

You don’t need to push through discomfort or have all the answers before beginning. To learn more, contact our team to arrange a complimentary phone consultation.
